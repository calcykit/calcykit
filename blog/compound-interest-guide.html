What is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and all previously accumulated interest. Unlike simple interest which only grows on the original amount, compound interest causes your money to grow exponentially — your returns generate their own returns.

Think of it this way. With simple interest $10,000 at 10% earns $1,000 every year — always calculated on the original $10,000. With compound interest that same $10,000 earns $1,000 in year one but $1,100 in year two because now you are earning 10% on $11,000. By year ten the difference is enormous.

The core mechanism: Interest earns interest. Every period your accumulated interest gets added to your principal and the next period's interest is calculated on that larger total. This cycle accelerates your growth exponentially over time rather than linearly.

The Compound Interest Formula

The standard compound interest formula used by banks, investment platforms, and financial institutions worldwide:

Compound Interest Formula
A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)

A = Final amount (principal + all interest earned)

P = Principal — your starting investment amount

r = Annual interest rate as a decimal (8% = 0.08)

n = Number of times interest compounds per year

t = Time in years

The interest earned alone is simply A minus P:

Interest Earned
Interest = A − P = P × ((1 + r/n)^(n×t) − 1)

Compounding Frequency — Does It Matter?

The more frequently interest compounds the more you earn. But the differences are smaller than most people think:

Key insight: The jump from annual to monthly compounding adds $607 over 10 years. The jump from monthly to daily adds only $57 more. Monthly compounding captures most of the benefit of frequent compounding — which is why savings accounts use monthly compounding as the standard.

The Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is the most useful mental math shortcut in investing. It tells you approximately how long it takes to double your money at any interest rate.

Rule of 72
Years to Double = 72 ÷ Interest Rate
At 8% annual return your money doubles in approximately 9 years (72 ÷ 8 = 9)
Annual ReturnYears to Double (Rule of 72)Actual Years
4%18 years17.7 years
6%12 years11.9 years
8%9 years9.0 years
10%7.2 years7.3 years
12%6 years6.1 years
15%4.8 years4.96 years

The Rule of 72 is remarkably accurate across all normal interest rate ranges. Use it to quickly evaluate any investment opportunity — if someone promises to double your money in 3 years that implies a 24% annual return (72 ÷ 3 = 24). Is that realistic? Probably not from a legitimate investment.

The Power of Starting Early — The Most Important Section

Nothing in finance is more important than this. Starting 10 years earlier can more than double your final wealth — even if you invest less total money.

Person A vs Person B

Both invest $5,000 per year at 8% annual return. Person A starts at age 25 and stops at 35 — investing for just 10 years. Person B starts at age 35 and invests every year until retirement at 65 — investing for 30 years.

Person A invested $100,000 less than Person B but ended up with $419,000 more. The only difference is 10 years of head start. Those early years of compounding are irreplaceable — no amount of catching up later can fully compensate for missing them.

The brutal truth about waiting: Every year you delay starting your investment costs you more than you realize. The money you invest at age 25 has 40 years to compound. The same money invested at 35 only has 30 years. That 10-year difference at 8% means the early money is worth 2.2x more at retirement than the later money.

How to Maximize Compound Growth

1. Start Immediately — Not Next Month

Even $100 today is worth more than $200 next year when compound interest is involved over long periods. The best time to start was yesterday. The second best time is right now.

2. Reinvest Every Single Penny

Never withdraw interest or dividends during your growth phase. Every dollar withdrawn breaks the compounding chain. Reinvestment is what turns compound interest from theory into life-changing wealth.

3. Maximize Your Rate — Minimize Fees

A 1% annual fee on a $100,000 portfolio compounding at 8% over 30 years costs over $125,000 in lost growth. Index funds with 0.03% to 0.1% expense ratios versus actively managed funds at 1-2% make an enormous long-term difference.

4. Add Regular Contributions

Compound interest on your principal is powerful. Compound interest on your principal plus regular monthly contributions is exponentially more powerful. Even $200 per month added to a $10,000 starting balance at 8% grows to over $300,000 in 20 years versus $49,000 with no contributions.

5. Choose Monthly or Daily Compounding

When choosing between savings accounts or investment vehicles offering different compounding frequencies — monthly beats quarterly beats annual. Daily versus monthly makes minimal difference but monthly versus annual is meaningful over decades.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?+

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and all previously accumulated interest. Unlike simple interest which only grows linearly on the original amount, compound interest grows exponentially — your returns generate their own returns creating accelerating growth over time.

What is the compound interest formula?+

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t), where P is principal, r is annual interest rate as a decimal, n is compounding frequency per year, and t is time in years. A is the final amount including principal and interest. Interest earned alone is A minus P.

What is the Rule of 72?+

The Rule of 72 is a mental math shortcut to estimate how long it takes to double your money. Divide 72 by the annual interest rate. At 8% your money doubles in approximately 9 years (72 ÷ 8 = 9). At 6% it takes 12 years. The rule is accurate across all normal interest rate ranges.

How often should interest compound for maximum growth?+

The more frequently interest compounds the more you earn. Daily compounding yields slightly more than monthly which yields more than annual. Monthly compounding captures most of the benefit and is the standard for savings accounts and most investment vehicles.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?+

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal every period. Compound interest is calculated on principal plus all previously earned interest. Over long periods the difference becomes enormous — $10,000 at 8% for 30 years grows to $34,000 with simple interest but over $109,000 with compound interest.
